1 Fluid Mechanics Foundations

Governing equations — what CFD actually solves.

Every airflow in the THADES system — the buoyant rise in the DSF cavity, the suction of the TST fan, the supply jet from the DOAS — obeys the same three conservation laws. CFD is, at bottom, a machine that solves these three simultaneously in every cell of a mesh.

The continuum assumption (why the equations can be written at all)

Before any conservation law can be written, one assumption must hold: that air can be treated as a continuum — a substance whose properties vary smoothly in space, rather than as discrete molecules. White grounds this precisely. Density is defined not as a fixed value but as a limit:

ρ = limδV → δV* (δm / δV)White, Fluid Mechanics (7th ed.), Eq. (1.1) — density as a limiting ratio of molecular mass δm to a small volume δV, taken down to a limiting volume δV* (≈ 10⁻⁹ mm³ for air at standard conditions, containing ~3×10⁷ molecules).

Below δV* the value fluctuates with individual molecules; above it, bulk variation appears; at δV* the density is well-defined and smooth. Why this matters for the research: the continuum assumption is what permits differential calculus — and therefore the governing equations and CFD itself — to be applied to the air in the DSF cavity, TST, and occupied zone. White notes it fails only at very low pressures where molecular spacing approaches the system size, which never occurs in a building. This is the licence on which the whole method rests, which is why it is stated first rather than assumed silently.

Conservation of mass (continuity)

Air entering a region must equal air leaving plus any accumulation. For the near-incompressible airflow in a building, this reduces to the statement that the velocity field has zero divergence — the flow neither compresses nor creates mass. This is what forces the DOAS supply and the fan exhaust to balance: air cannot be pushed in without an equal path out.

div u = 0Versteeg & Malalasekera, Eq. (2.5) — continuity for an incompressible fluid (ρ constant), reduced from the general mass-conservation Eq. (2.4). In vector-operator notation this is written ∇·u = 0; the two are identical.

The equation states that the divergence of the velocity field is zero: at every point, the mass of air flowing in equals the mass flowing out, with no local compression. Why this form is used in the research: building airflow moves far below the speed of sound, so density change is negligible and the incompressible form (2.5) is the correct simplification of the general continuity equation. It is not included as background — it is an active constraint the CFD solver enforces in every cell, and it is what couples the DOAS supply rate to the TST exhaust rate: a given mechanical supply cannot be resolved without an equal, physically consistent outflow path, which is precisely the balance the THADES active system must respect.

Conservation of momentum (Navier–Stokes)

This is Newton's second law applied to a fluid parcel: its acceleration equals the sum of forces — pressure gradients, viscous friction, and body forces such as gravity/buoyancy. The buoyancy term is the one that matters most here: when air warms, its density drops, and gravity acting on that density difference becomes an upward force. That single term is the physical origin of the stack effect in the TST and the recirculation that produces the aerodynamic short-circuit.

ρ (Du/Dt) = −∂p/∂x + div(μ grad u) + SMxVersteeg & Malalasekera, Eq. (2.34a) — x-component of the Navier–Stokes momentum equation for a Newtonian fluid; the y- and z-components have the same form. Du/Dt is the substantive derivative — the book's term (Eq. 2.7: Dφ/Dt = ∂φ/∂t + u·grad φ), also called the material or total derivative in other texts. SM is the momentum source term.

The left side is the rate of change of momentum following a fluid particle; the right side sums the forces acting on it — the pressure gradient, the viscous stress div(μ grad u), and any body force collected in SM. Why this equation is central to the research: it is the equation the CFD solver integrates to produce the velocity field, and two features of Versteeg's formulation matter directly for THADES. First, momentum is written per component using the substantive derivative, not as a compact vector expression — this is the form actually solved. Second, gravity and buoyancy are not written into the main equation; they enter through the source term SM (implemented via the Boussinesq treatment, Module 5). This is not a notational detail: the buoyancy source is the physical origin of the stack effect in the TST and of the recirculation that produces the aerodynamic short-circuit — the phenomenon the doctoral research sets out to resolve. The momentum equation is therefore the point at which the mechanism of RQ1 is expressed mathematically.

Conservation of energy

Thermal energy is transported by the flow (advection) and by conduction, with sources such as solar gain. For the incompressible air of a building (where internal energy i = cT and div u = 0), Versteeg recasts the general energy equation into a temperature equation:

ρc (DT/Dt) = div(k grad T) + SiVersteeg & Malalasekera, Eq. (2.25), reduced form — temperature form of the energy equation for an incompressible fluid, with the viscous-dissipation term omitted. ρc(DT/Dt) is the rate of change of thermal energy following the flow; div(k grad T) is conduction; Si is the energy source (e.g. solar gain). Viscous dissipation is neglected because building airflow is low-velocity, so frictional heating is negligible — a standard assumption for indoor CFD (confirm the precedent in the referenced studies, e.g. Yang; Chen & Gorlé).

The left-hand side, DT/Dt, carries temperature with the moving air (the same substantive derivative as in the momentum equation), which is precisely why this equation couples temperature to velocity. Why this equation completes the mechanism: a buoyancy problem cannot be solved as flow-only. Temperature drives density, density drives the buoyancy source in the momentum equation (SM), buoyancy drives the flow, and the flow then redistributes temperature through this very equation. The three conservation laws are therefore a closed loop — and the energy equation (2.25) is the link that closes it. For THADES this is not academic: the entire question of whether the DSF cavity's warm air short-circuits to the TST, or is instead flushed through the occupied zone by the DOAS, is a question about how temperature and velocity co-evolve, which only the coupled energy equation can answer.

Versteeg & Malalasekera, Intro to CFD (2nd ed.), §2.1–2.3 — the continuity (Eq. 2.5), substantive derivative (Eq. 2.7), and Navier–Stokes (Eq. 2.34a) forms used above.

It is a direct demonstration that this equation set, with a buoyancy source, reproduces measured multi-storey buoyant flow — the closest published evidence that the RQ1 mechanism can be captured at the equation level.

3 Heat Transfer & the CHTC

Conduction, convection, and the coefficient that links flow to energy.

Three modes move heat: conduction (through the solid walls and glazing), convection (carried by the moving air — the mode CFD resolves), and radiation (solar and surface-to-surface). The quantity that ties the CFD flow field to an energy result is the convective heat transfer coefficient (CHTC, α): it says how much heat a surface exchanges per degree of temperature difference, given the air moving past it.

CHTC is obtained from the Nusselt number, and — this is the key logic — which Nusselt correlation applies depends on the flow regime from Module 2. The chain is: CFD gives near-wall velocity → compute Re and Gr → form Ri → pick the regime → apply that regime's Nusselt correlation → get α.

α = Nu · λ / lYang et al. (Paper 2), Eq. (2) — CHTC from Nusselt number Nu, air conductivity λ, representative length l.

This equation converts a dimensionless Nusselt number into a physical heat transfer coefficient α, in W/m²K. Why it is the pivot of the whole method: α is the single quantity that turns a CFD airflow result into an energy result. Without it, a velocity field is just air movement; with it, that movement becomes a surface heat exchange that the building-energy model can add to its load balance. Every energy figure the doctoral research reports for THADES passes through this conversion, which is why the correct Nusselt correlation — selected by regime below — matters so much.

Natural convection (buoyancy dominant, Ri > 10):

NuN = 0.0241 (Gr·Pr)0.4Yang et al. (Paper 2), Eq. (3) — natural convection, vertical plane. Applies when the fan is off and the cavity/tower runs on buoyancy alone. Coefficient and exponent should be confirmed against the source, as they vary with surface geometry.

Forced convection (mechanical drive dominant, Ri < 0.1):

NuF = 0.037 Re0.8 Pr1/3Yang et al. (Paper 2), forced-convection regime (Ri < 0.1). This is the standard turbulent flat-plate average Nusselt correlation (valid 0.6 ≤ Pr ≤ 60), independently confirmed in Incropera & DeWitt — it is not the Dittus–Boelter pipe correlation (0.023).

Mixed convection (both, 0.1 ≤ Ri ≤ 10) — the two are blended:

NuM = (NuNn + NuFn)1/n, n = 2Yang et al. (Paper 2), Eq. (12) — mixed convection, combining natural and forced. This is the regime the THADES hybrid most often occupies, with passive buoyancy and active supply/exhaust acting together.

Why these three correlations, and not one: a single correlation would misrepresent THADES, because the system deliberately operates across regimes — buoyancy-only when passive, fan-driven when active, and both together in normal hybrid operation. The mixed-convection blend (Eq. 12) is therefore the expression that most often applies, and it is the one that lets the research quantify how the active supplementation changes the surface heat exchange relative to the passive baseline — the core comparison of RQ3.

4 Turbulence Modelling

RANS, LES, and why turbulence needs a model at all.

Turbulent flow contains eddies spanning a vast range of sizes. Resolving all of them directly (DNS) is impossible for a building-scale domain — the computational cost is astronomical. So turbulence is modelled, not fully resolved. But there is a deeper, more precise reason a model is unavoidable, which Pope sets out as the closure problem.

The closure problem (why a model is mathematically necessary, not just convenient). When the Navier–Stokes equations are time-averaged to give the Reynolds equations, the averaging introduces new unknowns — the Reynolds stresses (−ρ⟨u′iu′j⟩). Pope's argument is exact: for a three-dimensional mean flow there are four governing equations (three Reynolds-equation components plus continuity), but they now contain more than four unknowns, because the Reynolds stresses are additional. A system with more unknowns than equations is unclosed — it cannot be solved without supplying extra information. This is not a computational limitation; it is a mathematical one. A turbulence model is precisely the "extra information" that closes the system.

RANS (Reynolds-Averaged Navier–Stokes) solves for the time-averaged flow and closes the system through the Boussinesq eddy-viscosity hypothesis: it treats turbulent mixing as if it were an extra, much larger "turbulent viscosity". Models like k-ε and k-ω SST compute that turbulent viscosity from two transported quantities (turbulent kinetic energy and its dissipation/frequency). RANS is cheap and standard for buildings. LES (Large Eddy Simulation) resolves the large, energy-carrying eddies directly and models only the small ones — more accurate for unsteady, separated, or buoyant plumes, but far more expensive.

τij = −ρ u′iu′j = μt(∂Ui/∂xj + ∂Uj/∂xi) − ⅔ ρk δijVersteeg & Malalasekera, Eq. (3.33) — Boussinesq hypothesis: Reynolds stresses modelled via the turbulent (eddy) viscosity μt. Here k is turbulent kinetic energy per unit mass and δij is the Kronecker delta.

This is the closure that makes RANS tractable: instead of resolving every fluctuating stress, the turbulent mixing is bundled into a single quantity, the eddy viscosity μt, which models such as k-ε and k-ω SST then compute. Why this closure is used rather than resolving turbulence directly: directly simulating all turbulent scales (DNS) is computationally impossible at building scale, so a RANS closure is the practical choice — and the Boussinesq hypothesis (Eq. 3.33) is its foundation. It matters for THADES because μt is largest exactly in the shear layers around the TST fan jet and the cavity plume; the predicted flow in those regions — and therefore the predicted short-circuit — depends on the turbulence model chosen, making this closure a decision the research must justify, not a formality.

5 Boundary Conditions

How real devices — DOAS, fans, buoyancy — enter a CFD domain.

The central idea established earlier: CFD does not model a "DOAS" or a "fan" as an object — it models their effect as a condition on the boundary of the domain. The device becomes a specification of what the air does where it enters or leaves. Buoyancy, by contrast, is not a boundary at all: it acts throughout the domain, and enters through the momentum source term.

5.2 · How buoyancy actually enters the equations

Module 1 established that Versteeg does not write gravity into the momentum equation directly; it is carried in the source term SM. The book states the form explicitly:

SMx = 0  ·  SMy = 0  ·  SMz = −ρgVersteeg & Malalasekera, §2.1.3 — "the body force due to gravity would be modelled by SMx = 0, SMy = 0 and SMz = −ρg". Gravity acts only in the vertical (z) direction; ρ is density and g gravitational acceleration.

This is the exact mechanism by which buoyancy drives flow. Where the air is warm, ρ is lower, so the downward force −ρg is weaker than in the surrounding cooler air — the net imbalance pushes the warm air upward. Why this term is the physical heart of the research: the entire THADES stack effect, and the aerodynamic short-circuit it produces, originates in this single source term. If ρ were uniform, SMz would be uniform, no buoyant imbalance would exist, and there would be no stack flow and no short-circuit. The doctoral work therefore stands or falls on whether the CFD model represents this density–gravity coupling correctly.

5.3 · The Boussinesq approximation for buoyancy

The source term above requires that density vary with temperature. Letting ρ vary everywhere in every equation is expensive and can destabilise the solver. The Boussinesq approximation is the standard compromise used in indoor CFD: density is treated as constant in all terms except the gravity/buoyancy term, where it is allowed to vary linearly with temperature through the thermal expansion coefficient β:

ρ(T) = ρ̄ − ρ̄ β (T − T̄)Bird, Stewart, Lightfoot & Klingenberg, Introductory Transport Phenomena, Eq. (11.3-1) — named there explicitly as the Boussinesq approximation. β ≡ −(1/ρ)(∂ρ/∂T)p evaluated at T̄, the volumetric thermal expansion coefficient (1/K); ρ̄ and T̄ are reference values. The book derives it as a Taylor expansion of ρ(T) at constant pressure, truncated after the first two terms. Equivalent to the commonly written form ρ ≈ ρ0[1 − β(T − T0)]. The same β appears in the Grashof number, Gr = gβΔT l³/ν² (Yang, Paper 2, Eq. 4).

The crucial detail is where this relation is substituted. Bird states it precisely: Eq. (11.3-1) is inserted into the ρg (gravity) term of the momentum equation, but not into the ρ(Dv/Dt) inertia term. Density is therefore constant everywhere except in the buoyancy force. Making that substitution yields what the book calls the Boussinesq equation of motion:

ρ̄ (Dv/Dt) = (−∇p + ρ̄g) − [∇ · τ] − ρ̄ g β (T − T̄)Bird et al., Eq. (11.3-2) — the momentum equation with buoyancy substituted in. The final term −ρ̄gβ(T − T̄) is the buoyancy force; τ is the viscous stress tensor. This is the same physics as Versteeg's SMz = −ρg source term, written with the density–temperature relation already inserted.

Why this equation, and not a simpler one, matches the research: Bird notes that Eq. (11.3-2) covers forced convection, free convection, and the region between the two extremes. In pure forced convection the buoyancy term is neglected; in pure free convection the (−∇p + ρ̄g) term is dropped. THADES occupies neither extreme — passive buoyancy and active fan/DOAS operate together — so the full equation, with both terms retained, is the one that describes the system. This is the momentum-equation counterpart of the Richardson-number regime selection in Module 2 and the mixed-convection Nusselt blend in Module 3: three expressions of the same physical fact.

Why the validity limit matters here: Bird qualifies the approximation as successful "for free convection at moderate temperature differences," where the acceleration term Dv/Dt is small compared with g. Indoor air in a tropical office generally satisfies this. But the limit is not academic for THADES: the DSF cavity can reach substantially higher temperatures than the occupied zone under Jakarta solar loading. If the cavity–room temperature difference grows large, the linear truncation weakens. Gray and Giorgini (1976) provide the quantitative criterion — property variations should not exceed roughly 10% for the full approximation to hold. Establishing that the modelled temperature range stays within that bound, or moving to a variable-density formulation if it does not, is a modelling decision the research must make and justify rather than inherit from a solver default.

5.4 · Why passive and active must coexist in one domain

The two mechanisms are of different kinds: the DOAS and TST fan are boundary conditions imposed at the edges of the domain, while buoyancy is a volumetric source acting inside it — the −ρ̄gβ(T − T̄) term of Eq. (11.3-2), present in every cell where the air is warmer or cooler than the reference. They are solved simultaneously, and their interaction is what the research must resolve: whether the fan's suction reinforces the buoyant path (and so accelerates the short-circuit) or redirects it through the occupied zone.

Neither can be studied in isolation. Dropping the buoyancy term — the forced-convection simplification Bird describes — would delete the very phenomenon under investigation. Dropping the pressure/mechanical terms — the free-convection simplification — would delete the intervention being tested. The THADES model must therefore retain the full form, which is precisely why the system sits in the mixed regime rather than at either limit.

6 Mesh, Discretisation & Validation

Making a CFD result trustworthy.

CFD turns the continuous equations of Module 1 into algebra by dividing the domain into a mesh of finite cells and approximating the equations on each — the finite-volume method. Two separate disciplines make the output credible, and they are routinely confused:

Verification asks "am I solving the equations right?" — a purely numerical question about whether the discretisation has converged. Validation asks "am I solving the right equations?" — a physical question about whether the model corresponds to reality. A perfectly grid-converged result can still be physically wrong if the turbulence model or the boundary conditions are wrong. The two must be established separately.

6.1 · Verification: quantifying discretisation uncertainty

The common practice is to refine the mesh until the answer "stops changing" and declare grid-independence. Roache's objection is that this is a subjective judgement: the observed difference between two grids, ε, depends on how much the grid was refined (the refinement ratio r) and on the order of the numerical method (p). A 5% difference from a barely-refined grid and a 5% difference from a doubled grid do not carry the same meaning at all. Reporting ε alone is therefore not an error estimate.

The Grid Convergence Index puts all such studies onto a common basis by converting any (r, p) result into the equivalent uncertainty that a grid-doubling with a second-order method would have produced:

GCIfine grid = Fs · |ε| / (rp − 1)Roache, Verification and Validation in Computational Science and Engineering, Eq. (5.6.1). ε is the relative difference between the two grid solutions (Eq. 5.4.3); r is the grid refinement ratio; p is the observed order of convergence; Fs is a factor of safety. Roache's default is Fs = 3; he discusses Fs = 1.25 as a less conservative alternative when more grids support the estimate. With Fs = 1, the GCI reduces to the Richardson error estimator E₁.

Why this is computed rather than merely asserted: the GCI is a number the research will calculate and report, not a concept operating behind the solver. It converts grid-independence from a claim into a stated uncertainty band on the result — for instance, that a predicted flow rate carries a 6% discretisation uncertainty. Because the integrated THADES configuration has no built prototype to measure against, the credibility of the mechanism findings rests disproportionately on this kind of internal rigour: an examiner who cannot check the result against a physical measurement can still check whether the discretisation uncertainty was quantified and reported.

6.2 · Near-wall resolution and its link to the CHTC

Mesh quality is not uniform in importance across the domain. The convective heat transfer coefficient of Module 3 is governed by the thin boundary layer at each surface, where velocity and temperature gradients are steepest. Resolving that layer requires either a very fine near-wall mesh or a wall function that models it. This is not a general refinement issue but a targeted one: an under-resolved boundary layer corrupts α directly, and through α, every energy figure the research reports. The mesh strategy and the CHTC method are therefore a single coupled decision, not two independent ones.

6.3 · Validation without a prototype

Validation compares the model against measurement. The difficulty for this research is structural: the integrated THADES system — DSF, TST, external louver, DOAS and mechanical exhaust operating together — has never been built, so no measurement of the whole system exists. The response is a tiered strategy: validate the components and the physics against cases that have been measured, then assemble. Buoyancy-driven cavity flow, stack-driven extraction, and mechanically-driven room ventilation each have published, measured precedents; the integrated configuration is then a composition of validated parts rather than an unvalidated whole. This is a defensible position, but it must be argued explicitly rather than assumed.

The literature supplies both the template for this approach and the component benchmarks it requires. The dominant pattern across these studies is consistent: a measurable case — most often a scaled physical model or an experimental chamber — is used to validate the CFD setup (turbulence model, wall treatment, boundary conditions), and only then is that validated setup applied to the configuration of real interest. 7 Coupling & Reduced-Order Methods

BES–CFD coupling, surrogate models, and sensitivity analysis.

BES–CFD coupling

BES (EnergyPlus/DesignBuilder) is fast and good at annual energy and control logic, but treats a room as one well-mixed node — it cannot see local airflow. CFD sees the airflow in detail but is too slow for a year. Coupling lets each supply what the other lacks: BES passes surface temperatures to CFD; CFD passes back near-wall velocity (or a CHTC), which BES uses to correct its heat balance. This is the method behind RQ3.

Surrogate models

Because CFD is too slow to run the thousands of times that optimisation and sensitivity need, a surrogate is trained: a machine-learning model that learns the input→output relation from a limited set of CFD runs, then predicts new cases almost instantly. It is a calculating machine, not a lookup table — it computes predictions for combinations never simulated. Common types, in rising data appetite: polynomial models, radial basis functions (RBF), and Kriging (Gaussian process).

Forrester gives the concrete reason Kriging is usually preferred when CFD samples are scarce. Its basis function is:

ψ(i) = exp( − Σj=1..k θj | xj(i) − xj |pj )Forrester, Sóbester & Keane, Engineering Design via Surrogate Modelling, §2.4, Eq. (2.19) — the Kriging basis function. θj is the per-variable width and pj the per-variable exponent; k is the number of design variables.

Forrester frames this by direct contrast with the Gaussian radial basis function of the preceding section. Where a Gaussian basis uses a single width (1/σ²) applied to every variable, the Kriging basis carries a vector θ = (θ₁, θ₂, …, θk), allowing the width to differ from one variable to the next. And where the Gaussian exponent is fixed at 2, the Kriging exponent is itself a vector p = (p₁, …, pk), typically with pj ∈ [1, 2], free to vary per dimension. When the exponent is fixed at 2 and θ is held constant across all dimensions, the Kriging basis reduces exactly to the Gaussian — so the Gaussian is a special case, and the two extra vectors are what give Kriging its added flexibility. Why this matters for the research: the THADES design variables are not equally influential — cavity depth, tower geometry, and louver gap act on very different scales — and the per-variable width θ lets the surrogate stretch or compress each dimension to match its true influence, which a single-width basis cannot. This is the concrete basis for preferring Kriging over a plain radial basis function or a polynomial when CFD samples are expensive and few.

Check from source / decide by experiment The choice of surrogate (Kriging, radial basis function, or another form) and the minimum number of CFD samples required are project-specific — to be determined from Forrester's guidance and from accuracy tests (RMSE against held-out cases), not assumed in advance.

Sobol sensitivity analysis

Once the surrogate is trained and its accuracy verified (RMSE against held-out CFD cases), Sobol analysis quantifies how much of the output's variance each variable — and each interaction — is responsible for. It runs by generating many input combinations (Saltelli sampling) and calling the surrogate for each. It produces two indices per variable:

Si = VXi[ EX∼i(Y | Xi) ] / V(Y)Saltelli et al., Eq. (1.25) — first-order sensitivity index: the share of output variance V(Y) explained by variable Xi acting alone. Always between 0 and 1. The total-effect index STi (Saltelli §1.2.12, "Total Effects") adds all interactions involving Xi.

The numerator measures how much the output's expected value moves as Xi is varied; dividing by the total variance V(Y) expresses it as a proportion. Why this index, and not a simpler measure, is required: the doctoral optimisation involves many geometric variables that may interact, and a one-at-a-time sensitivity test cannot detect interactions. The Sobol decomposition can, which is why it is chosen. Two consequences follow directly for THADES. First, the keep-or-fix decision for a variable rests on its total index STi (a variable is fixed only if STi ≈ 0), so that a variable which matters only through interaction is not discarded. Second — and this is a caveat, not a convenience — the standard index assumes independent inputs, whereas the master's glazing SHGC and U-value were confounded; Saltelli's treatment of non-independent factors is therefore directly relevant, and the analysis must account for it rather than ignore it.

A crucial caveat: Sobol assumes independent inputs. The master's glazing SHGC and U-value were confounded (locked as Type 1/Type 2), so standard Sobol cannot separate them — the "non-independent input factors" section of Saltelli addresses exactly this.
